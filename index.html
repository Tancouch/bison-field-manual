THE FOUR COMPONENTS OF A PAYMENT

When a client says "my payment," they mean the whole thing. When a rate sheet says "payment," it means principal and interest only. That gap is where most bad expectations are born.

P&I
Principal and interest. The only part the rate controls. Fixed for the life of a fixed-rate loan.
T
Property taxes. Set by the county, not the lender. Reassessed on sale in many states.
I
Homeowners insurance. Set by the carrier. Rising fastest of the four in most markets.
MI
Mortgage insurance. Applies below twenty percent down on most programs. Removable on some, not all.
Add HOA separately. Dues are not escrowed and not in the payment, but they are absolutely in the client's debt-to-income calculation. A four hundred dollar association fee can cost a buyer roughly sixty thousand dollars of purchase power.

THE ONE NUMBER: DEBT TO INCOME

Approval is mostly a debt-to-income question. Take the client's total monthly obligations, including the new housing payment, and divide by gross monthly income. Most agency programs are comfortable to roughly forty-five percent, and will stretch higher with strong compensating factors such as reserves or a large down payment.

What counts as debt: the new PITI, HOA dues, car payments, student loans, minimum credit card payments, child support and alimony, and any co-signed obligation. What does not: utilities, phone, insurance premiums outside the escrow, groceries, or a loan with fewer than ten payments remaining on most programs.

The most valuable thing you can do before writing an offer: ask the client whether anything about their debts has changed since pre-approval. A financed truck bought during escrow has killed more closings than appraisals have.

READING THIS OUT LOUD

Quote the total, then break it. Clients who hear only principal and interest feel misled at closing, and they blame whoever quoted it. Clients who hear the full number up front trust the next number you give them.

On mortgage insurance: conventional mortgage insurance is temporary and comes off. That difference is worth real money over ten years and is the single most common thing agents get wrong.

Acquisition debt capInterest is deductible on the first $750,000 of debt used to buy, build, or substantially improve the home, or $375,000 filing separately. Loans originated on or before December 15, 2017 are grandfathered at $1,000,000.

PART 06Advisor Track

THE BALANCE
SHEET VIEW

Four conversations that belong to the advisor, not the agent.

1. HOW MUCH SHOULD THE CLIENT PUT DOWN

The reflex answer is twenty percent, to avoid mortgage insurance. The correct answer depends on what the marginal dollar earns elsewhere and what the mortgage insurance actually costs.

Frame it as a spread. If a client puts an extra fifty thousand dollars down, the guaranteed return on that dollar is the mortgage rate, after tax if they itemize. Compare that to the expected after-tax return on the same dollar invested, adjusted for the client's risk tolerance and their liquidity needs. Then add the cost of the mortgage insurance they avoid, which is real but temporary on conventional financing.

The trap: a client who puts every liquid dollar into the down payment and closes with no reserves. Reserves are both an underwriting factor and the thing that keeps a new roof from becoming a credit event.

2. DOCUMENTING A BUSINESS OWNER'S INCOME

This is where advisors add the most value, and where the most deals die. A client whose CPA has spent five years minimizing taxable income has, on paper, minimized their borrowing power. Agency underwriting uses net income after expenses, not gross revenue or distributions.

If a purchase is likely within twenty-four months, that is a conversation to have with the CPA before the return is filed, not after. Where the tax strategy should not change, the answer is a bank statement, profit and loss, or asset-based program. See Part 03.

Bank statement

Twelve or twenty-four months of deposits, with an expense factor applied. Best when revenue is strong and write-offs are aggressive.

Asset depletion

Qualifying income derived from liquid and retirement assets. Best for a retiree or a client whose wealth is not income.

DSCR

Investment property only. Qualifies on the property's rent against its payment. No personal income used at all.

3. WHEN THE HOUSE IS THE CHEAPEST MONEY AVAILABLE

A client with substantial equity and a funding need has several options, and the mortgage is not always the wrong one. A closed-end second or a line of credit preserves a low first-mortgage rate that a cash-out refinance would destroy. Compare the blended rate of keeping the first and adding a second against the rate on a new larger first before recommending either.

Where interest is deductible: only when the borrowed funds are used to buy, build, or substantially improve the residence securing the loan. Proceeds used to invest or pay off consumer debt do not qualify under the acquisition debt rules, regardless of what the loan is secured by.

4. THE RETIREMENT PURCHASE

A retiree with seven figures in assets and forty thousand dollars of reported income is declinable on a standard agency file and easily approvable on an asset-based one. Retirement distributions can be used as qualifying income when there is documented continuance, and assets can be converted to income under asset depletion. Neither requires the client to actually start drawing down the account.

The referral that works. Advisors do not need to explain a program. They need to recognize that one exists. If a client's situation looks impossible on paper, that is the signal to call, not the signal to stop.

THE FIVE MOMENTS THAT DELAY A CLOSING

New credit during escrow

A financed vehicle, a furniture account, or a card opened after pre-approval. Credit is re-pulled before closing on nearly every file.

Large unsourced deposits

Any deposit that is not payroll must be documented. Gift funds need a letter and a paper trail. Cash is not documentable.

Appraisal below contract

The loan is sized off the lower of price or appraised value. The gap is renegotiated, covered in cash, or the contract is cancelled.

Condo project review

The building has to qualify, not just the buyer. Litigation, investor concentration, and reserve shortfalls all stop a file.

Insurance binding

In wildfire and wind exposed markets, getting a carrier to bind can now take longer than underwriting the loan. Start it at contract.

A job change

Employment is verified again within days of closing. A resignation, a switch to 1099, or a new bonus structure all require re-underwriting.
